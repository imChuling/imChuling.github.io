Overview

Finding the right timbre for a melody has always slowed me down. I wanted a colour picker for sound, but a timbre alone does not carry a performance: the vibrato, the slides and the way each note swells come from the player. Quill is a sampler built to capture both.

From a short monophonic recording, Quill builds two independent, reusable assets: a timbre and a note-level behavior library, so that how an instrument sounds and how it is played can be mixed and matched. A MIDI melody can pair any library with any compatible timbre without retraining. I designed and built it on my own as a desktop instrument with real-time MIDI input and one-click behavior capture.

Existing tools keep the two tied together. A recording stores an instrument’s sound and one performance together, and a sampler replays it. Timbre transfer is driven by live audio, so its expression cannot be redirected to new notes. MIDI-DDSP generates expression inside a model, for the instruments in its training data. I have not found a system that stores a way of playing as an asset that a new melody can reuse with a different sound.

System architecture

Quill has three layers. One take can feed all three: a spectral timbre that plays at once, a behavior library if the take has at least two notes, and a DDSP timbre if it lasts 8 seconds or more. Every timbre takes the same two control signals, f0 and loudness, so a behavior library can drive any of them without a learned adapter.

Spectral core (real time). This is the timbre you play live. PESTO estimates the fundamental frequency (f0). On an STFT I measure up to 60 harmonic amplitudes per frame by parabolic peak interpolation and store the residual as a 40-band noise envelope. An additive synthesizer written in NumPy and Numba resynthesizes the sound from MIDI and resamples the spectral envelope at the target harmonics, which preserves formants under transposition. Sixteen voices render a 256-sample block in a median of 2.65 ms (budget 5.8 ms, Apple M2).

Behavior library. This is where a way of playing is stored, note by note. Each entry holds four 48-point trajectories (pitch deviation, energy, brightness, noisiness), a label (plain, vibrato or slide) and six context features: pitch, duration, the gaps before and after, and phrase-initial and phrase-final flags.

Neural timbre (offline). This version is for rendering and export. Each instrument can have a DDSP harmonic-plus-noise model. An MLP and GRU decoder with 494,718 parameters maps f0 and loudness to 60 harmonic and 65 noise-band amplitudes at 16 kHz.

Between the layers. Pitch deviation sets f0 and energy modulates loudness. With the neural timbre and in live playing, the two assets exchange only these two signals. Offline rendering with a captured spectral timbre also uses the brightness and noisiness curves.

Pipeline

Segment and label. pYIN tracks f0. Note boundaries are the union of detected onsets and f0 steps, so a legato note change with no new attack is still split. Rules on each note’s pitch contour assign its label: a range under 30 cents is plain, a mostly one-way drift above 60 cents is a slide, and four or more direction changes make a vibrato.

Retrieve. Each MIDI note borrows the gesture of a similar recorded note. Articulation-conditioned nearest-neighbor retrieval filters entries by label and selects the one with the smallest Euclidean distance over the standardized context features. By default the app then reranks the eight nearest candidates for smoother joins between notes.

Adapt. The chosen trajectories are resampled to the note duration at 250 frames per second. When a note is much longer or shorter than its source, whole vibrato cycles are added or removed to keep the recorded rate. Label rules then shape the pitch: plain notes are low-passed at 3 Hz, vibrato notes lose drift below 2 Hz, and slides take their interval from the score.

Render. The adapted f0 and loudness drive the chosen timbre. Notes inside a phrase join with a 20 ms loudness crossfade. Live playing uses the spectral core, and rendering and WAV export can use either the spectral timbre or a DDSP model.

Using Quill

Capture records up to 60 seconds from the microphone, an audio file, or one stem that Demucs separates from a full song. Texture mode takes unpitched sounds such as rain or wind, as a timbre only. The spectral timbre is playable at once, and one save stores both assets.

On the keyboard, a gesture interpreter turns overlapping keys within two semitones into legato and wider overlaps into slides. Three notes within 80 ms become a strum, and the mod wheel sets vibrato depth. When a behavior library drives live playing instead, each key press retrieves a trajectory, with the note length estimated from recent playing. Brightness applies a spectral tilt of up to 6 dB per octave, Air / Noise sets the level of the noise layer, and an LFO adds vibrato at 2 to 10 Hz.

In the piano roll, each note carries a label: plain, vibrato, slide or legato. Playback renders the notes with the current timbre and behavior pair, and the result exports as MIDI or WAV. Baking a take into a DDSP timbre takes about four minutes on an Apple M2.

Tech stack

  • Real-time engine: Python with NumPy and Numba, sounddevice for audio, mido and python-rtmidi for MIDI. PyTorch and librosa stay outside the audio callback.
  • Analysis: PESTO and pYIN for f0, librosa for spectral features, Demucs for stem separation.
  • Neural timbre: DDSP in PyTorch, trained with a multi-scale spectral loss.
  • App: a pywebview desktop window with an HTML and JavaScript interface, and a local FastAPI render service.

Evaluation and limitations

The evaluation asks whether timbre and behavior really stay separate, and whether listeners can hear it. Cross-rendering violin, trumpet and guitar passed 12 of 12 source-attribution checks: behavior features (pitch modulation, pitch drift, RMS) followed the behavior source and MFCC features followed the timbre source. In a pre-registered study with 19 listeners, all 19 rated retrieved behavior more human-like than no behavior, and 109 of 114 cross-instrument ABX responses identified the behavior source.

To check that the label itself drives the result, I rendered each of 29 notes twice, changing only its label from plain to vibrato. Vibrato-band energy rose by a median of 7.0 times in the control signal and 4.7 times in the audio. Most of that contrast comes from the label-specific clean-up rules: with them switched off, the control-signal ratio fell to 1.55. The glass timbre model and its 24-note library were both built from about 82 seconds of rubbed-glass recordings from the decent|SAMPLES Wine Glasses library.

Naturalness is the main limitation. MIDI-DDSP was rated more natural (median 5 against 4, on different melodies). On one melody, 16 of 19 listeners rated a parametric behavior model more natural than retrieval. Quill connects notes that the player detached: the loudness dip at a note change has a median of 10.9 dB in the violin recordings and 6.6 to 8.0 dB in Quill’s renders of three test phrases. Retrieval has no condition for note connection. Behavior libraries and DDSP timbres need pitched, monophonic sources, and the neural output is 16 kHz.

Reflection

A lower error is not a better performance. Against held-out playing, the average curve for each label had a lower error than retrieval, and no vibrato at all. So I judge behavior by listening and by how note boundaries are distributed, as well as by error.

Retrieval earns a narrower claim than I first expected. Listeners could tell whose playing a render carried, even on another instrument, yet a parametric model sounded more natural. To show what retrieval keeps that a vibrato oscillator cannot, I would fix the melody and timbre and compare a parametric vibrato with two different players.

Some playing transfers between instruments and some does not. A predictor trained on violin, using the gaps between notes, found where a trumpeter detaches notes about as well as one trained on trumpet (AUC 0.86 against 0.84), but its error on detachment depth rose from 7.6 to 11.7 dB. Next I would add note connection as a fourth retrieval condition, set by the musician or read from the gaps in the MIDI, with its depth calibrated to the receiving timbre.

Quill: Learning Timbre and Performance Behavior as Separate, Swappable Assets

Chuling Li

ISMIR 2026 Late-Breaking Demo · Abu Dhabi, UAE

An expressive renderer must learn two things: how an instrument sounds and how it is played. Quill factorizes them into two separately learned, reusable assets: a lightweight DDSP timbre model and a note-level style library of pitch and energy trajectories, retrieved by articulation-conditioned nearest-neighbor lookup. Either asset can be swapped across instruments without retraining. Cross-rendering across violin, trumpet, and guitar passes all 12 source-attribution checks. In a pre-specified listening study (N=19), listeners track behavior across timbres and rate renders that carry it as more human-like. A paired counterfactual test isolates the articulation-label intervention from note-level confounds. A case study shows that both assets can also be learned from 82 s of rubbed-glass recordings.

Overview

I’ve always wished for a kind of magic. I’d take a timbre I like and put it on the way another instrument is played. Quill is my first prototype of that wish. It keeps the playing separate from the sound. You record a few seconds of a pitched sound and get two things: a timbre you can play from a keyboard, and a library of how the notes in that recording were shaped.

Then you can mix them. Play a melody, choose the glass as the timbre and a violin recording as the behavior, and the glass picks up the violinist’s vibrato. Switch the timbre to trumpet and the same vibrato follows. Nothing is retrained when you change the pairing.

How it works

Capturing a sound. PESTO tracks the pitch, with pYIN as a fallback. On a short-time Fourier transform I find each harmonic’s peak and use parabolic interpolation to measure its level, keeping up to 60 harmonics per frame. I subtract those harmonics from the spectrum and keep what’s left as a 40-band noise envelope and a short attack. Stored this way, the sound can be transposed and stretched. The synthesizer resamples the spectral envelope, so the formants stay where they were. Quality drops as a note moves far from the register it was recorded in.

Describing how notes were played. For a recording with several notes, pYIN gives a pitch curve. I split it into notes at detected onsets and at steps in the pitch, which catches legato notes that have no new attack. Simple rules on the pitch curve label each note plain, vibrato or slide. For each note I store four 48-point curves: pitch deviation, energy, brightness and noisiness. I also store six things about where it sat: pitch, length, the gaps before and after, and whether it starts or ends a phrase.

Playing a new melody. For each note you write, Quill takes the recorded notes with the same label and picks the nearest one by Euclidean distance on those six features, each scaled by its spread in the library. Editing a note’s length can select a different recording. Once a source note is selected, Quill adapts its curves to the target length. Where the pitch curve has clear vibrato cycles, it adds or removes whole cycles to keep the recorded rate. A few rules then clean up: a 3 Hz low-pass removes leftover vibrato from plain notes, drift below 2 Hz is removed from vibrato notes, and slides take their interval from the score. The pitch curve becomes f0 and the energy curve shapes the loudness.

Making sound. Both sound engines are driven by the same two curves, f0 and loudness. For live playing I wrote an additive synthesizer in NumPy and Numba that plays the captured sound from a MIDI keyboard. For rendered audio, an instrument can have a small DDSP model: an MLP and GRU decoder with about 495,000 parameters that turns f0 and loudness into 60 harmonic levels and 65 noise bands at 16 kHz. I trained these with a multi-scale spectral loss. Because both engines take the same two inputs, any behavior library can drive any of these timbres without retraining. With the neural timbres only pitch and loudness travel between them, so what a player does to the tone itself, such as bow noise or breath, stays behind.

In the app. The desktop app has capture from a microphone or a file, MIDI input and a piano roll. Two menus, TIMBRE and BEHAVIOR, set the pairing, so you can hear the same notes in different combinations. You can mark any note as vibrato or slide. When you play live, overlapping keys become legato or a slide, and the mod wheel sets vibrato depth.

A captured sound is playable straight away. SAVE also extracts a behavior library if the take has several notes, and BAKE DDSP can fine-tune a neural timbre in the background. Live playing uses the captured sound. Rendered playback and WAV export use the neural one.

Evaluation and reflection

Spectral modeling and DDSP are existing methods. What I added is the behavior library: playing detail stored as its own asset, which a new score can call on with any compatible timbre. So the first thing to check was whether the two really stay separate.

I cross-rendered violin, trumpet and guitar in every mixed pairing. In 12 of 12 checks, the pitch-modulation features followed the behavior source and the MFCC features followed the timbre source.

Then I ran a randomized listening study with 19 people. All 19 found the version with retrieved behavior more human-like than the same melody without it. 18 of 19 found the version with vibrato and slide labels more expressive than an all-plain one. When asked to tell which instrument's behavior was driving a cross-rendered clip, listeners were correct in 109 of 114 trials across six instrument pairings (each 17 to 19 out of 19, all statistically significant after Bonferroni correction). I also compared retrieval with an earlier parametric model that rebuilt each vibrato from a few numbers, such as rate and depth. On one lyrical melody, 16 of 19 judged the parametric version more natural (p = 0.004, two-sided).

I also rendered each note twice, changing only its label from plain to vibrato and keeping the synthesis noise the same. Energy in the vibrato band was 7 times higher in the control curves and 4.7 times higher in the audio. With those rules switched off, the control contrast fell to about 1.5. In a separate test, I held each of 16 violin source notes fixed and stretched it to 1.8 times its duration. In the trumpet audio, cycle-based retiming reduced the median vibrato rate error from 2.38 to 0.40 Hz.

The glass is the smallest case. About 82 seconds across 13 recordings gave both a timbre model and a 24-note behavior library.

Natural playing, a realistic instrument and a useful new sound are different goals. A glass played with a violinist’s vibrato isn’t meant to sound like a real instrument, but the playing should still hang together. On that measure, Quill doesn’t sound as natural as a real player yet. In the same study, MIDI-DDSP scored higher on violin naturalness (median 5 against 4) on different melodies. I tried rendering with the player’s own curves in place of retrieved ones, and it didn’t sound more natural. What I did hear, against the original recordings, was that Quill joins notes the player had separated. The numbers agree: at a note change a real violin’s loudness dips by about 11 dB, and Quill’s renders dip by 7 or 8. That listening was only me, on three phrases, so it tells me where to look and not much more.

Along the way I learned that a lower error isn’t the same as a better performance. The average curve for a label had the lowest error against real playing, and no vibrato at all. I kept retrieval because it lets me reuse the movement of a recorded note. Which details listeners recognize beyond vibrato rate and depth is still open.

Next, I’d let retrieval tell connected notes from detached ones, set by you or read from the gaps in the MIDI, and test it with more listeners and more pieces. For now Quill works on pitched, monophonic sounds, the neural output is 16 kHz, and the app is still a prototype. The scope is narrow on purpose. I wanted to hear one idea clearly before widening it.

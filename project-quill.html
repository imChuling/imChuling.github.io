Overview

Quill is a desktop instrument that lets musicians reuse the sound of an instrument and the way it is played independently. From a short monophonic recording, it captures two assets: a timbre and a library of note-level behaviors. A new MIDI melody can pair any behavior library with any compatible timbre, without retraining.

A sampler keeps sound and performance together and plays them back. Timbre transfer follows live audio, and MIDI-DDSP generates expression inside a model for the instruments it was trained on. Quill stores the way of playing as an asset that new melodies and other timbres can reuse. I designed and built it independently, including real-time MIDI input and one-click behavior capture.

System architecture

One take can feed three assets: a spectral timbre that plays at once, a behavior library when the take has two or more notes, and a DDSP timbre when it lasts 8 seconds or more. Every timbre takes the same controls, f0 and loudness, so any behavior library can drive it without a learned adapter.

Spectral core. PESTO estimates f0. Parabolic peak interpolation on an STFT measures up to 60 harmonics per frame, and the residual becomes a 40-band noise envelope. An additive synthesizer in NumPy and Numba plays it from MIDI, resampling the spectral envelope so formants stay in place. Sixteen voices render a 256-sample block in a median of 2.65 ms (budget 5.8 ms, Apple M2).

Behavior library. Each note stores four 48-point trajectories (pitch deviation, energy, brightness, noisiness), a label (plain, vibrato or slide) and six context features: pitch, duration, the gaps before and after, and phrase position.

Neural timbre. For rendering and export, a DDSP harmonic-plus-noise model (MLP and GRU decoder, 494,718 parameters) maps f0 and loudness to 60 harmonic and 65 noise-band amplitudes at 16 kHz.

Pipeline

Segment and label. pYIN tracks f0, and note boundaries combine onsets with f0 steps so legato note changes are split too. A range under 30 cents is plain, a mostly one-way drift above 60 cents is a slide, and four or more direction changes make a vibrato.

Retrieve. Articulation-conditioned nearest-neighbor retrieval gives each MIDI note the closest recorded note with the same label, by Euclidean distance over the standardized context features. The app then reranks the eight nearest for smoother joins.

Adapt. Trajectories are resampled to the note length at 250 frames per second, adding or removing whole vibrato cycles to keep the recorded rate. Label rules finish the pitch: a 3 Hz low-pass for plain notes, drift removal below 2 Hz for vibrato, and the score’s interval for slides.

Render. f0 and loudness drive the chosen timbre, with a 20 ms crossfade between notes. In offline rendering, a spectral timbre also follows the brightness and noisiness curves.

Using Quill

Capture takes up to 60 seconds from the microphone, a file, or one stem that Demucs separates from a song. Texture mode keeps unpitched sounds such as rain as a timbre only. One save stores both assets.

On the keyboard, a gesture interpreter plays overlapping keys within two semitones as legato and wider overlaps as slides, turns three notes within 80 ms into a strum, and maps the mod wheel to vibrato depth. With a behavior library selected, each key press retrieves a trajectory instead. Brightness, Air / Noise, the envelope and an LFO shape the tone. In the piano roll, each note takes a label, playback uses the current timbre and behavior pair, and the result exports as MIDI or WAV. Baking a DDSP timbre takes about four minutes on an Apple M2.

Tech stack

  • Real-time engine: Python with NumPy and Numba, sounddevice and python-rtmidi. PyTorch and librosa stay out of the audio callback.
  • Analysis: PESTO, pYIN, librosa and Demucs.
  • Neural timbre: DDSP in PyTorch with a multi-scale spectral loss.
  • App: pywebview with an HTML and JavaScript interface, and a local FastAPI render service.

Evaluation

Separation. Across violin, trumpet and guitar, all 12 of 12 source-attribution checks passed: pitch modulation, drift and RMS followed the behavior source, and MFCCs followed the timbre source.

Listening. In a pre-registered study with 19 listeners, all 19 rated retrieved behavior more human-like than none, and 109 of 114 cross-instrument ABX answers identified the behavior source.

The label’s effect. Rendering 29 notes twice, changing only plain to vibrato, raised vibrato-band energy by a median of 7.0 times in the control signal and 4.7 times in the audio. The label rules produce most of it: without them the ratio is 1.55.

Small data. The glass timbre and its 24-note library come from about 82 seconds of rubbed glass (decent|SAMPLES Wine Glasses). Held-out takes reconstruct at a spectral loss of 7.1, against 12.1 to 13.2 through the violin and trumpet models.

Findings and next steps

Naturalness depends on how notes connect. Listeners rated MIDI-DDSP more natural (median 5 against 4, on different melodies), and on one melody 16 of 19 found a parametric behavior model more natural than retrieval. Compared with the recordings, the difference lies between notes: violinists dip a median of 10.9 dB at a note change, Quill’s renders 6.6 to 8.0 dB. Retrieval does not yet condition on note connection.

Where playing transfers. A violin-trained predictor finds where a trumpeter detaches notes as well as a trumpet-trained one (AUC 0.86 against 0.84), while the depth of detachment stays instrument-specific (error 11.7 against 7.6 dB). The next step is note connection as a fourth retrieval condition, with its depth calibrated to the receiving timbre.

Error is not the target. The average curve for each label has a lower error than retrieval and no vibrato, so behavior is judged by listening and by how note boundaries are distributed.

Scope. Behavior libraries and DDSP timbres need pitched, monophonic sources, and the neural output is 16 kHz.

Quill: Learning Timbre and Performance Behavior as Separate, Swappable Assets

Chuling Li

ISMIR 2026 Late-Breaking Demo · Abu Dhabi, UAE

An expressive renderer must learn two things: how an instrument sounds and how it is played. Quill factorizes them into two separately learned, reusable assets: a lightweight DDSP timbre model and a note-level style library of pitch and energy trajectories, retrieved by articulation-conditioned nearest-neighbor lookup. Either asset can be swapped across instruments without retraining. Cross-rendering across violin, trumpet, and guitar passes all 12 source-attribution checks. In a pre-specified listening study (N=19), listeners track behavior across timbres and rate renders that carry it as more human-like. A paired counterfactual test isolates the articulation-label intervention from note-level confounds. A case study shows that both assets can also be learned from 82 s of rubbed-glass recordings.

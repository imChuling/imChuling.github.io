Overview

I’ve always wished for a kind of magic. I’d take any timbre I like and put it on the way any instrument is played, or even the way any sound moves. Quill is my first prototype of that wish. It keeps the playing separate from the sound. You record a few seconds of a pitched sound and get two things: a timbre you can play from a keyboard, and a library of how the notes in that recording were shaped.

Then you can mix them. Play a melody, choose the glass as the timbre and a violin recording as the behaviour, and the glass picks up the violinist’s vibrato. Switch the timbre to trumpet and the same vibrato follows. Nothing is retrained when you change the pairing.

How it works

Capturing a sound. PESTO tracks the pitch, with pYIN as a fallback. On a short-time Fourier transform I find each harmonic’s peak and use parabolic interpolation to measure its level, keeping up to 60 harmonics per frame. I subtract those harmonics from the spectrum and keep what’s left as a 40-band noise envelope and a short attack. Stored this way, the sound can be played at any pitch and length. The synthesiser resamples the spectral envelope, so the formants stay where they were.

Describing how notes were played. For a recording with several notes, pYIN gives a pitch curve. I split it into notes at detected onsets and at steps in the pitch, which catches legato notes that have no new attack. Simple rules on the pitch curve label each note plain, vibrato or slide. For each note I store four 48-point curves: pitch deviation, energy, brightness and noisiness. I also store six things about where it sat: pitch, length, the gaps before and after, and whether it starts or ends a phrase.

Playing a new melody. For each note you write, Quill takes the recorded notes with the same label and picks the nearest one by Euclidean distance on those six features, each scaled by its spread in the library. It stretches that note’s curves to the new length. A few rules then clean up: a 3 Hz low-pass removes leftover vibrato from plain notes, drift below 2 Hz is removed from vibrato notes, and slides take their interval from the score. The pitch curve becomes f0 and the energy curve shapes the loudness.

Making sound. Those two curves, f0 and loudness, are all a timbre needs. For live playing I wrote an additive synthesiser in NumPy and Numba that plays the captured sound from a MIDI keyboard. For rendered audio, an instrument can have a small DDSP model: an MLP and GRU decoder with about 495,000 parameters that turns f0 and loudness into 60 harmonic levels and 65 noise bands at 16 kHz. I trained these with a multi-scale spectral loss. Because both engines take the same two inputs, any behaviour library can drive any timbre.

In the app. The desktop app has capture from a microphone or a file, MIDI input and a piano roll. Two menus, TIMBRE and BEHAVIOR, set the pairing, so you can hear the same notes in different combinations. You can mark any note as vibrato or slide. When you play live, overlapping keys become legato or a slide, and the mod wheel sets vibrato depth.

A captured sound is playable straight away. SAVE also extracts a behaviour library if the take has several notes, and BAKE DDSP can fine-tune a neural timbre in the background. Live playing uses the captured sound. Rendered playback and WAV export use the neural one.

Evaluation and reflection

Spectral modelling and DDSP are existing methods. What I added is the behaviour library: playing detail stored as its own asset, which a new score can call on with any timbre. So the first thing to check was whether the two really stay separate.

I cross-rendered violin, trumpet and guitar in every mixed pairing. In 12 of 12 checks, the pitch-modulation features followed the behaviour source and the MFCC features followed the timbre source.

Then I ran a randomised listening study with 19 people. All 19 found the version with retrieved behaviour more human-like than the same melody without it. 18 of 19 found the version with vibrato and slide labels more expressive than an all-plain one.

I also rendered each note twice, changing only its label from plain to vibrato and keeping the synthesis noise the same. Energy in the vibrato band was 7 times higher in the control curves and 4.7 times higher in the audio. Most of that contrast comes from the clean-up rules. I know because I switched them off and it fell to about 1.5.

The glass is the smallest case. About 82 seconds across 13 recordings gave both a timbre model and a 24-note behaviour library.

Quill doesn’t sound as natural as a real player yet. In the same study, MIDI-DDSP scored higher on violin naturalness (median 5 against 4). I tried rendering with the player’s own curves in place of retrieved ones, and it didn’t sound more natural. What I did hear, against the original recordings, was that Quill joins notes the player had separated. The numbers agree: at a note change a real violin’s loudness dips by about 11 dB, and Quill’s renders dip by 7 or 8. That listening was only me, on three phrases, so it tells me where to look and not much more.

Along the way I learned that a lower error isn’t the same as a better performance. The average curve for a label had the lowest error against real playing, and no vibrato at all. That’s why I kept retrieval: it returns a note somebody actually played.

Next, I’d let retrieval tell connected notes from detached ones, set by you or read from the gaps in the MIDI, and test it with more listeners and more pieces. For now Quill works on pitched, monophonic sounds, the neural output is 16 kHz, and the app is still a prototype.

01 / The project

What is HARP?

HARP lets musicians use different AI models through one application, standalone or alongside their DAW. It loads each model’s controls for tasks such as separating vocals, transcribing audio to MIDI, or generating musical variations. Models run locally or in the cloud, and results return to HARP for playback, saving, or sending back to the DAW.

It is part of TEAMuP, an NSF-funded collaboration between the University of Rochester and Northwestern University. The initiative is led by Raffaella Borasi, alongside Zhiyao Duan, Rachel Roberts, Jonathan Herington, and Bryan Pardo.

02 / My work

Making the models usable

Across 26 music and audio AI models, I worked on integration, deployment, and validation. I adapted research code with PyHARP and investigated failures in model loading, inputs, and outputs. This work involved questioning assumptions in both the models and the tests used to assess them.

I also traced validation failures to unsuitable test inputs and missing text-output support, submitting configurations for six models and fixes to the shared validator. My observation that DDSP-Guitar was being tested with piano MIDI prompted instrument and channel configuration, implemented by Frank Cwitkowitz and merged into HARP.

03 / Reflection

What this changed for me

Every model brings musical assumptions

Working across models showed me that a shared interface still contains different assumptions about music. Input duration, instrument settings, and output formats shape what a model can do. The DDSP-Guitar mismatch made this concrete: a technically valid MIDI file could represent the wrong instrument. I learned to ask what musical situation each test actually represents.

Turn observations into evidence

Restoring MIDI-VAE taught me to trace a plausible output back to its weights and equations before trusting it. Raising the guitar-input issue taught me to make a finding reproducible and explain its relevance so a teammate could build on it. Together, these experiences strengthened how I investigate problems and contribute to shared research software.

The research question I want to pursue

How should music AI tools communicate their capabilities, limits, and controls so musicians can make informed creative decisions? HARP led me to this question; my work so far establishes functional reliability. I would next explore it through listening sessions and complete DAW tasks, examining how musicians understand a model’s controls and whether its results support their intentions.

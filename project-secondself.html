Second Self

Independent Project · Ongoing Interactive Vocal Performance Max for Live · FluCoMa Real-Time Audio

An automated performance system in Max for Live: a singer sings with phrases of herself from earlier in the same performance.

Overview

Second Self grew out of the friction of operating software while performing. It is a vocal performance system built as three Max for Live devices: it records and slices the vocal in real time, recalls earlier phrases according to the chord being played, and keeps them in time with Ableton Live. Once the take starts, I don’t touch the computer.

The idea comes from a short dialogue in the Qiwulun chapter of the Zhuangzi. The Penumbra asks the Shadow why it keeps walking and stopping with no will of its own, and the Shadow says it doesn’t know. Maybe it depends on something, and that something depends on something else. I think of the second voice as that shadow. Everything it sings is my own voice from the same performance, yet the system decides when it comes in and which of my earlier takes it brings back. Across the four acts it goes from following me to leading me. By the end I am thinking of the butterfly dream, where Zhuang Zhou wakes up and can’t tell whether he dreamed the butterfly or the butterfly is dreaming him.

It all runs on the CPU of an M2 MacBook Air, with no neural models.

Other systems do parts of this. OMax, Somax2 and DYCI2 recombine a performer’s material for improvisation, often with someone steering them. The Reflexive Looper lines up a performer’s recordings with a chord grid and picks what to do based on how they are playing. I haven’t found much that brings these together inside a fixed song form, using only material from that night, with nobody at the controls.

System architecture

Listen and slice. While I sing, my voice is recorded into a buffer and cut wherever a new sound starts, into pieces between 0.4 and 4 seconds long (FluCoMa). For each piece the system works out a 12-bin chroma vector, a Tonal Interval Vector that sums up its harmony, and six simple descriptors such as loudness, brightness and duration. It also splits off the percussive part, the consonants and breath, and keeps it in a second buffer.

Index by place in the song. The backing is a four-bar chord loop, and a silent MIDI clip of triads tells the system which chord is playing. Every piece is filed under the chord it started on, how many milliseconds into that chord it began, and where it falls in the verse form (A, A, AB, AB).

Recall in place. When a phrase comes back, it comes back over the same chord and at the same point in the bar, so it lands on the harmony and on the beat. The pieces of one phrase play back together as a whole line. If no chord clip is running, the system searches by harmony instead: a k-d tree (k = 5) finds the pieces whose Tonal Interval Vector is closest to what I just sang, ranked by how much they clash with it.

Fit by taking away. A phrase won’t always fit the chord that is playing now, so each one gets a conflict score between 0 and 1. That score sets how strongly a spectral mask (8192-point FFT) pulls down the partials outside the chord, by up to about 9 dB. I use the same score for space as well. The worse a phrase fits, the further out to the side it sits, and the darker and more reverberant it sounds.

Place and move. Each returning voice has its own place: right beside me, off to one side, or far away. Voices also move while they sing. A backwards line in a rest drifts in toward the middle, the canon crosses to the other side, and in the last act the lead voice slowly backs away.

A little chance. Where the system picks something by score, like the interval for a harmony part or the next note of the inner voice, it sometimes takes the second-best option, as long as it scores almost as well. That happens about one time in ten while it stays close to me and three in ten once it is on its own, so the same spot in the loop doesn’t always get the same answer.

Performance structure

The four acts follow the verses of the song. Each act changes which part of the verse the second voice takes from, counted from where I am. The system moves on to the next act by itself, based on how long the take has run, how much I have sung, how dense and loud my singing is, and how long I stay silent.

Tech stack

  • Host: Ableton Live 12 with three Max for Live devices (Max 9).
  • Analysis: FluCoMa for onset slicing, chroma, harmonic-percussive separation, nearest-neighbor search, and pitch and loudness tracking.
  • Decisions: JavaScript inside Max, with 175 offline tests in Node and a simulated full take.
  • Audio: MSP patching: eight playback voices, an FFT mask, an eight-line feedback delay reverb, and an output limiter.
  • Show control: before a take, a status line on the device names the first thing that isn’t connected yet (input, clock, transport, chord clip). A mute can be mapped to a foot switch.
  • Logging: OSC to CSV, one row per recall.

Evaluation and limitations

So far I have tested it as an instrument. I haven’t run a study with listeners yet.

  • The decision logic and the patch files pass 175 offline tests. I also run a simulated take of the whole song that prints every return by loop, bar and source verse. In that take no more than four of the eight voices ever sound at once, and none has to be cut off to make room for another.
  • In Max 9, with the interval set to 0, the pitch shifter switches out completely and the output cancels exactly against the patch without it. Its latency is a constant 4096 samples (85 ms at 48 kHz), so shifted phrases are started that much earlier.
  • There was a click at the start of some phrases. It came from message order: in 4 of 40 recalls the gain opened 1 to 8 ms before the playhead had jumped. Sending the gains last fixed it.
  • I rendered the full output chain in Max 9 for 64 recalls across all four acts. Nothing went above 0.98, there were no invalid values, and every phrase start, ending and voice change faded in or out smoothly.
  • On loudspeakers the act II shadow can feed back through the microphone. I fed its output back into the recording to see how bad it gets. At a loop gain of 0.7 it died out within a second, at 1.4 it rang for 3 to 5 seconds, and at 2.8 it ran away. At soundcheck the system now measures the loop gain in the room and turns the shadow down from 0.25 and off from 0.5.

Limitations:

  • The responses are worked out live, during the take, from what I have just sung and the chord that is playing: which phrase returns, at what interval, and which note the inner voice takes. The rules that make those choices are written by hand, and nothing is learned from data. They are tuned to one song with a fixed loop and verse form.
  • The chords come from a MIDI clip. The system doesn’t listen to the backing track itself.
  • The pitch shifter has no formant correction, so I only ever shift my recorded voice down.
  • The act II shadow lands about 130 ms behind me. That is an estimate: 40 ms of read offset plus the shifter’s latency.
  • Onset detection sometimes cuts a long held note in two.
  • On loudspeakers the microphone also picks up the second voice, so it ends up in the memory and comes back later. Only isolation fixes that: in-ear monitoring and a close microphone.
  • The inner-voice line, the movement in space and the shortening in act IV have only been tested offline. I haven’t rehearsed them in a full take yet.
  • I haven’t run a study with listeners or other performers. There is an ablation mode for comparing retrieval strategies, and I haven’t used it yet.

Reflection

My first full take changed a lot. The second voice was too quiet, and mostly it just repeated what I had sung a moment before. Now a returning phrase is sung at an interval picked for that phrase, and each act takes its material from a different distance in the verse.

I have also learned not to trust tests alone. Bringing back only the consonants in the rests passed every test and sounded like noise when I rehearsed it, so it is switched off. An octave double, a spectral freeze and comb resonators were built and then taken out after listening. Later, rendering the chain in Max showed that the harmony switch turned itself off every time the device loaded, and no test had caught it. Now after every rule change I run the simulated take, render the chain, and then listen.

The question I am still working on is how a performer can anticipate and steer a partner like this with nothing but her voice. Next I want to rehearse the newer layers, record a full performance, and run the ablation with listeners.

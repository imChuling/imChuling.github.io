Second Self

Independent Project · Ongoing Interactive Vocal Performance Max for Live · FluCoMa Real-Time Audio

An automated performance system in Max for Live: a singer sings with phrases of herself from earlier in the same performance.

Overview

Second Self grew out of the friction of operating software while performing. It is a vocal performance system built as three Max for Live devices: it records and slices the vocal in real time, recalls earlier phrases according to the chord being played, and keeps them in time with Ableton Live. Once the take starts, I don’t touch the computer.

The idea comes from a short dialogue in the Qiwulun chapter of the Zhuangzi. The Penumbra asks the Shadow why it keeps walking and stopping with no will of its own, and the Shadow says it doesn’t know. Maybe it depends on something, and that something depends on something else. I think of the second voice as that shadow. Everything it sings is my own voice from the same performance, yet the system decides when it comes in and which of my earlier takes it brings back. Across the four acts it goes from following me to leading me. By the end I am thinking of the butterfly dream, where Zhuang Zhou wakes up and can’t tell whether he dreamed the butterfly or the butterfly is dreaming him.

OMax, Somax2 and DYCI2 already do some of this: they recombine a performer’s material to create improvisations, often with a person guiding the process. The Reflexive Looper matches recorded phrases to a chord grid and responds to how the musician is playing. But I’ve found few systems that bring these approaches together within a fixed song form, using only material from that night’s performance and running without anyone at the controls.

System architecture

Listen and slice. As I sing, the system records my voice into a buffer. FluCoMa detects the start of each new sound and divides the recording into segments lasting between 0.4 and 4 seconds. Each segment is analyzed for its 12-bin chroma vector, a Tonal Interval Vector describing its harmonic content, and six basic features, including loudness, brightness and duration. The system also separates out percussive sounds, including consonants and breath, and stores them in a second buffer.

Index by place in the song. The backing follows a four-bar chord loop. A silent MIDI clip supplies the chord information as a sequence of triads. Each segment is tagged with the chord it began on, its start time within that chord in milliseconds, and its position in the verse form (A, A, AB, AB).

Recall in place. Recalled phrases return over the same chord and at the same point in the bar. Their segments stay together, so the listener hears a complete line with its original timing. Without a chord clip, the system switches to a harmonic search: a k-d tree (k = 5) retrieves segments with Tonal Interval Vectors close to what I have just sung, then ranks them by how much they clash with it.

Fit by taking away. Some recalled phrases will clash with the current chord. Each receives a conflict score from 0 to 1, which controls a spectral mask using an 8192-point FFT. The mask reduces partials outside the chord by up to about 9 dB. The same score shapes the phrase’s spatial treatment: greater conflict pushes it further to the side and gives it a darker sound with more reverb.

Place and move. Returning voices occupy different positions around me, from close by to off to one side or in the distance. Those positions can change during a phrase. A reversed line moves toward the center during a rest, a canon drifts toward the opposite side, and the lead voice gradually recedes in the final act.

A little chance. The system does not always choose the highest-scoring option. When selecting a harmony interval or the next note of an inner voice, it can take the runner-up if the scores are close enough. This happens roughly 10% of the time while it stays close to me, rising to 30% once it is on its own. Repeated points in the loop can therefore produce different responses.

Performance structure

The four acts follow the verses of the song. Each act changes which part of the verse the second voice takes from, counted from where I am. The system moves on to the next act by itself, based on how long the take has run, how much I have sung, how dense and loud my singing is, and how long I stay silent.

Tech stack

  • Host: Ableton Live 12 with three Max for Live devices (Max 9).
  • Analysis: FluCoMa for onset slicing, chroma, harmonic-percussive separation, nearest-neighbor search, and pitch and loudness tracking.
  • Decisions: JavaScript inside Max, with 175 offline tests in Node and a simulated full take.
  • Audio: MSP patching: eight playback voices, an FFT mask, an eight-line feedback delay reverb, and an output limiter.
  • Show control: before a take, a status line on the device names the first thing that isn’t connected yet (input, clock, transport, chord clip). A mute can be mapped to a foot switch.
  • Logging: OSC to CSV, one row per recall.

Evaluation and limitations

So far I have tested it as an instrument.

  • The decision logic and the patch files pass 175 offline tests, and a simulated take of the whole song prints every return by loop, bar and source verse.
  • I rendered the full output chain in Max 9 for 64 recalls across all four acts. Nothing went above 0.98, and every phrase start, ending and voice change faded in or out smoothly.
  • On loudspeakers the act II shadow can feed back through the microphone. I measured where it starts to ring, and at soundcheck the system now measures the loop gain in the room and turns the shadow down or off.

Limitations:

  • The responses are worked out live from what I have just sung and the chord that is playing, by rules I wrote by hand for this song’s loop and verse form.
  • The chords come from a MIDI clip. The system doesn’t listen to the backing track itself.
  • The newest layers have only been tested offline, and I haven’t yet run a study with listeners.

Reflection

My first full take changed a lot. The second voice was too quiet, and mostly it just repeated what I had sung a moment before. Now a returning phrase is sung at an interval picked for that phrase, and each act takes its material from a different distance in the verse.

I have also learned not to trust tests alone. Bringing back only the consonants in the rests passed every test and sounded like noise when I rehearsed it, so it is switched off. An octave double, a spectral freeze and comb resonators were built and then taken out after listening. Later, rendering the chain in Max showed that the harmony switch turned itself off every time the device loaded, and no test had caught it. Now after every rule change I run the simulated take, render the chain, and then listen.

The question I am still working on is how a performer can anticipate and steer a partner like this with nothing but her voice. Next I want to rehearse the newer layers, record a full performance, and run the ablation with listeners.
